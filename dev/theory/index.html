How it works

This page summarizes the model and the numerical method. Each section points to where it is implemented.

The DAE

A power system with machines, controllers and loads is a semi-explicit differential-algebraic system:

\[\begin{aligned} \dot x &= f(x, y, v) && \text{machine and controller dynamics} \\ 0 &= g(x, y, v) && \text{device algebraic equations (stator, governor outputs, ...)} \\ 0 &= Y v - I(x, y, v) && \text{network: current balance at every bus} \end{aligned}\]

  • x: differential states, for example the rotor angle $\delta$, speed $\omega$, and internal EMFs $e_q', e_d'$ of each machine.
  • y: algebraic device states.
  • v: bus voltages in rectangular form $v_b = v_{r,b} + j v_{i,b}$.
  • $Y$: the network admittance matrix.

The network equation is written in current-injection form: each device injects a current $I_b(x, y, v)$ at its bus, and Kirchhoff's law gives $Y v = I$. A ZIP load with impedance part $y_L$ injects $-y_L v_b - \overline{(S_L / v_b)}$. A fault with impedance $r + jx$ injects $-v_b / (r + jx)$.

Initialization

runpf! solves the steady state $Y v = \overline{S / v}$ at given generation and load. It enforces generator reactive limits by switching PV buses to PQ. initialize_dynamics! then inverts each device's equations at that operating point. It finds machine internal states that inject exactly the power-flow current, and controller setpoints (governor reference, exciter voltage reference) that hold them there. The result satisfies $f = 0$ and $g = 0$ to machine precision, so an undisturbed simulation stays flat.

Time stepping

With step $h$, backward Euler (BE) and Crank–Nicolson (CN) both lead, at every step, to one nonlinear system in $z_{k+1} = (x, y, v)_{k+1}$:

\[F(z) = \begin{bmatrix} x - a - c\, f(z) \\ g(z) \\ Y v - I(z) \end{bmatrix} = 0, \qquad (a, c) = \begin{cases} (x_k,\ h) & \text{BE} \\ (x_k + \tfrac h2 f(z_k),\ \tfrac h2) & \text{CN} \end{cases}\]

Both methods share the same Newton machinery and only differ in the anchor $a$ and the coefficient $c$. The Jacobian is

\[J = \begin{bmatrix} I - c\,f_x & -c\,f_y & -c\,f_v \\ g_x & g_y & g_v \\ -I_x & -I_y & Y - I_v \end{bmatrix}.\]

Events (a fault switching on, a line tripping, ...) change $F$. They are applied between steps, followed by a re-solve of the algebraic equations ($c = 0$), so the next step starts from a consistent state. This handles the jump in voltages at a fault.

Newton's method and its cost

Each step runs Newton iterations $J \Delta z = -F(z)$. For large networks the sparse LU factorization of $J$ dominates. Two cheap savings:

  • Modified Newton: reuse the factorization across iterations and steps while convergence is fast ($\|F\|$ drops by more than ×10 per iteration).
  • Predictor: start from the extrapolation $2z_k - z_{k-1}$.

On ACTIVSg2000 together they cut factorizations from about 1200 to about 10 per 5 s simulation.

Structure: clusters and Schur reduction

Order the unknowns by cluster: each machine together with its governor, exciter and stabilizer, and finally the bus voltages. A cluster only touches the voltage of its own bus, so $J$ has an arrow shape:

\[J = \begin{bmatrix} A_1 & & & B_1 \\ & \ddots & & \vdots \\ & & A_N & B_N \\ C_1 & \cdots & C_N & D \end{bmatrix}, \qquad S = D - \sum_{k} C_k A_k^{-1} B_k .\]

  • The blocks: each $A_k$ is small and dense. $B_k$ and $C_k$ couple the cluster to its bus.
  • Elimination: eliminating the clusters leaves the Schur complement $S$. It has exactly the sparsity of the admittance matrix $Y$, because each correction lands on one bus's diagonal block.
  • One Newton step then costs:
    1. $N$ independent small dense LUs, which batch perfectly on a GPU;
    2. one sparse LU of a network-sized matrix;
    3. back-substitution.

This is solver = :schur on the CPU and integrate_gpu_schur_cudss! on the GPU. :schur_gmres instead solves the reduced system iteratively, preconditioned by the network admittance alone, which never needs refactoring.

Kernels and layout

Device equations are not written per device instance. Each device type stores its instances as a structure of arrays (a column per parameter). One kernel evaluates the residual, and one the Jacobian entries, for all instances of that type. The same kernels run on the CPU and, through KernelAbstractions.jl, on the GPU. The Jacobian's sparsity pattern is built once, and each kernel writes into precomputed positions of its nonzeros, so assembly allocates nothing.
